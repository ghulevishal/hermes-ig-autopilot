Your agent cannot tell you whether it worked

Across 19 unattended runs of one pipeline, 8 completed, 7 failed, and 4 ended in a state where nobody could say whether the side effects had happened. Here is the verification pattern that fixes it, and why your own log is the wrong place to look.

analysis2026-10-16·3 min read·on Instagram

An agent finishes a task and reports success. You believe it, because what else is there?

That report is a claim about what the agent attempted. Whether the thing actually happened is a separate fact, and the two come apart more often than you would expect.

Here is the run history of one small, unattended pipeline — a job that publishes a post twice a day:

`` completed 8 failed 7 unknown 4 <- side effects unverifiable ``

Nineteen runs. Four of them ended in a state where the system genuinely could not say whether the work had been done. The process was killed after the side effect but before the record of it, so both "it posted" and "it didn't" were consistent with the evidence.

Worse: the pipeline's own log claimed 11 posts existed. The platform API said 10. The log had been the thing I was trusting.

Why "done" is unreliable

Three failure shapes, all of which produce a confident success report:

The call returned, the write didn't land. HTTP 200 means the request was accepted, not that the downstream commit succeeded. Plenty of APIs acknowledge then fail asynchronously.

The process died between the act and the record. This is the classic dual-write problem. The side effect is in one system, the record of it is in another, and there is no transaction spanning both. A crash in the gap produces exactly the "unknown" state above.

A retry repeated completed work. The first attempt finished and died before logging. The retry has no idea, so it does it again. Now you have two posts, two charges, two emails.

None of these are exotic. All three happened in nineteen runs of a hobby pipeline.

Read-back verification

The pattern is old and boring, which is why it works: after an action with side effects, re-read the result from the system of record and assert on it.

Concretely, instead of:

`` result = publish(post) log("published", result.id) # trusting the return value ``

do:

`` result = publish(post) check = api.get_media(result.id) # ask the platform, not yourself assert check.id == result.id log("verified", check.id, check.permalink) ``

The difference is where the truth comes from. The first version records what your code believes. The second records what the outside world confirms.

Three rules make this practical:

The system of record is never your own log. Your log records intent; the external system records reality. When they disagree, your log is the one that is lying. If you find yourself reconciling two of your own files, you have not verified anything.

Assert on a named property. "It worked" is not checkable. "This id resolves and returns a permalink" is. Pick something specific enough to fail.

Unverifiable is its own status. If the read-back fails, the correct report is unknown, not done and not failed. Collapsing unknown into either one destroys the information you most need — an agent that reports success it cannot prove is more dangerous than one that fails loudly, because it teaches you to stop checking.

Where to put it

Put verification at the boundary where the side effect leaves your process. Not at the end of the run — by then you may be in a different process with different state.

And make the agent's final report a statement about what it verified, not what it attempted. "Published and confirmed id 18120… resolves" is a different sentence from "published successfully," and only one of them is falsifiable.

The uncomfortable generalisation

This gets worse as you add agents. A subagent reporting "done" to a parent is the same unverified claim, one level up, and the parent usually has even less ability to check. Multi-agent systems compound self-report error unless somebody, somewhere, reads back from the real system.

The fix is cheap. One extra read per side effect, and a willingness to let a run say "I don't know."
